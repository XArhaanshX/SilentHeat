SILENTHEAT - PRESENTATION SCRIPT

==========================================================================
1. WHAT IS OUR PROJECT                                   [Slides 1-5, ~1.5 min]
==========================================================================

[Slide 1 - Title]
Good morning. Our project is called SilentHeat. It is about a blind spot in how India
warns people about heat.

[Slide 2 - Problem statement]
India declares a heatwave using only the thermometer. On the plains it has to cross 40 °C,
on the coast 37 °C, and it has to be at least 4.5 °C above normal. That declaration is what
switches on the Heat Action Plans: water points, changed work hours, hospital readiness.

But the human body cools itself by sweating, and sweat only works if it can evaporate.
In humid air it can't. Scientists measure this with the wet-bulb temperature, which combines
heat and humidity. Lab studies show healthy young adults start losing control of their body
temperature at around 30-31 °C wet-bulb.

So a 36 °C day at 60% humidity in Kolkata or Chennai is physically dangerous, but because
it's below 40 °C, the official rule treats it as a normal day. No alert goes out.

[Slide 4 - The gap]
Most Heat Action Plans use these fixed national cut-offs and ignore humidity completely.

[Slide 5 - Our project]
So we asked six questions. How much dangerous humid heat does the rule miss? Is it getting
worse? Are the extremes moving? Does it come in multi-day spells? Can a simple probability
model warn a day ahead? And finally, can machine learning warn further ahead, even for
cities it has never seen?

Short answer: the official rule misses 96% of dangerous humid-heat days.

==========================================================================
2. NOVELTY                                               [Slides 20, 22, ~1.5 min]
==========================================================================

[Slide 20 - RQ6 set-up and what is new]
Three things make this project new.

One - we judge heat the way the body feels it, not the way the thermometer reads it.
Our ML model gets physiology-based features: how far today's wet-bulb is from the 29 °C
danger limit (we call it "headroom"), how fast that gap is closing, and roughly how many
days are left before it's crossed. Headroom came out as the 5th most useful of 24 features.

Two - we don't replace the IMD rule, we add to it. Our hybrid alert is simply:
"IMD rule OR our ML model". We score it on how much of the blind spot it recovers.
The IMD rule alone catches 1% of the danger days it can't see. The hybrid catches 45%.

Three - it works in cities with no history. We hid each city completely from training,
trained on the others, and predicted the hidden city. The model scored AUC 0.93 on cities
it had never seen, almost the same as 0.94 on cities it was trained on. That matters because
most Indian cities don't have decades of labelled heat records.

[Slide 22 - Novelty results]
And to be honest about the cost: about 64% of hybrid alerts are false alarms. That's fine
for a cheap "drink water, rest in the afternoon" advisory, but not for something like
closing schools.

==========================================================================
3. TECHNICAL IMPLEMENTATION (AND WHERE R IS USED)        [Slides 7-9, 12-21, ~2 min]
==========================================================================

[Slide 7 - Pipeline]
The whole project is built in R, end to end. One command, "Rscript run_all.R", rebuilds
everything from raw data in about a minute and a half.

[Slide 8 - Data]
Data: 45 years (1981-2025) of daily weather for 10 cities from ERA5 reanalysis, downloaded
through the Open-Meteo API. That's 164,360 city-days.

Where R is used, step by step:

- DATA DOWNLOAD (R, jsonlite): R calls the weather API with retries and caches the files.

- FEATURES (R, dplyr): R works out each day's normal temperature with harmonic regression,
  applies the official IMD heatwave rule to every single day, and flags every day with
  wet-bulb >= 29 °C.

- RQ1, BLIND SPOT (R): miss rate with a Wilson 95% confidence interval, bootstrap and Bayesian
  checks, plus chi-square, McNemar and Cohen's kappa. Result: 703 of 730 danger days missed,
  96.3%, CI 94.7-97.4%. Kappa is about zero - the two definitions basically never agree.

- RQ2, TRENDS (R, MASS): Mann-Kendall trend test and negative-binomial regression.
  Danger days grow 1.54 times every decade.

- RQ3, EXTREMES (R, written from scratch): we coded the GEV extreme-value likelihood
  ourselves and checked it against the evd package. Chennai's once-in-50-years humid extreme
  from 1985 now comes back roughly every 2.6 years.

- RQ4, SPELLS (R): a two-state Markov chain plus Monte Carlo simulation. Danger arrives in
  multi-day spells - in Chennai the chance of a 3-day spell each season went from 2% to 23%.

- RQ5, WARNING (R, pROC): logistic regression trained on 1981-2014, tested on 2015-2025
  which it never saw. AUC 0.92 against 0.61 for the IMD rule, and it's calibrated:
  when it says 70%, danger follows about 70% of the time.

- RQ6, MACHINE LEARNING (R, rpart + ranger): three baseline models - logistic regression,
  a decision tree and a random forest - forecasting 1, 2 and 3 days ahead.
  [Slide 21] The random forest wins at every lead time: AUC 0.95 one day ahead and still
  0.89 three days ahead. A single decision tree is the weakest; averaging 400 trees fixes that.

- REPORT (R, knitr): the written report pulls every number live from the result tables.

- DASHBOARD (R Shiny): the interactive app I'll show you now.

About 2,000 lines of R in total. The only non-R piece is a small Python script that builds
these slides from R's output tables, so the slides always match the analysis.

==========================================================================
5. CONCLUSION                                            [Slides 24-25, ~1 min]
==========================================================================

[Slide 24 - Conclusions]
To sum up:
- India's heatwave rule misses 96% of dangerous humid-heat days, in every city we studied.
- Those days are growing about 54% per decade, and the extremes are shifting in 7 of 10 cities.
- They come in multi-day spells, which is exactly when the body suffers most.
- A simple calibrated model warns far better than the rule, and a random forest extends the
  warning to three days ahead and to cities with no history at all.

Our recommendations: add a humidity-aware trigger, like wet-bulb or Heat Index, to heatwave
declarations; use local, probability-based thresholds instead of one national number; and
prioritise Kolkata, Bhubaneswar, Visakhapatnam and Chennai, where silent days dominate.

[Slide 25 - Limitations]
Limitations, quickly: ERA5 is a 25 km grid, so it smooths local peaks - real station data would
show even more danger days. And the physiological limits come from young healthy adults;
older people and outdoor workers are at risk sooner. Next step is feeding the model the
free 7-day weather forecast so it can issue real alerts ahead of time.

==========================================================================
LIKELY QUESTIONS - QUICK ANSWERS
==========================================================================
Q: Why wet-bulb 29 °C and not 35 °C?
A: 35 °C is the old theoretical limit. Lab tests (Vecellio 2022) found real people fail at
   30-31 °C, and 29 °C is a standard "dangerous" band used in research (Raymond 2020).

Q: Why only 5 cities in the ML part?
A: The other 5 (Delhi, Ahmedabad, Nagpur, Mumbai, Vellore) almost never reach 29 °C wet-bulb
   on the ERA5 grid, so there is nothing to predict there.

Q: Isn't 64% false alarms too high?
A: For a low-cost advisory it's acceptable - missing a real danger day costs far more than
   an unnecessary "drink water" message. And the threshold can be tuned for fewer alarms.

Q: How do you know you're not overfitting?
A: Everything is tested on 2015-2025, years the models never saw during training, and the
   alert thresholds were picked on 2010-2014 only.

Q: Why use ERA5 and not IMD station data?
A: IMD station data isn't freely available at this scale. ERA5 actually makes our numbers
   conservative - station data would show more danger days, not fewer.
